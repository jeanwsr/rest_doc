# Grid-Shift Derivatives via the Becke Partition in DFT

This document addresses the grid-shift derivative problem.

:::{note}
**This document treats only the closed-shell case**

For open-shell systems, each grid-shift term corresponds term by term to the closed-shell case: terms linear in $f_g^\chi$ (vxc) (such as `de_becke_full_1`, `de_becke_vxc_*`, `vmat_becke_dw`, `vmat_becke_vxc`) are summed over the two spin components; terms quadratic in $f_g^{\chi \chi'}$ (fxc) (such as `de_fxc`, `de_becke_atom_1`, `de_becke_atom_3`, `vmat_becke_fxc`) are summed over the four spin pairs $\alpha\alpha / \alpha\beta / \beta\alpha / \beta\beta$. The grid-weight part (the `dw`/`ddw` contractions of the Becke partition) is spin-independent; the $f_g \rho_g$ contracted with $\mathrm{ddw}$ in `de_becke_full_2` is taken as the spin-summed density.
:::

## 1. Overview of Grid-Shift Derivatives

### 1.1 The Relation of Grid Coordinates and Weights to Atomic Coordinates

Recall that for any function of the electronic coordinate $F(\boldsymbol{r})$, the numerical grid integration expression is

$$
E = \int F(\boldsymbol{r}) \, d\boldsymbol{r} \simeq \sum_{g} w_g F(\boldsymbol{r}_g)
$$

We now need to add complexity little by little. In gradient problems, we note that the function $F$ is often at the same time a function of the atomic coordinates $\boldsymbol{R}_A$. Thus we have

$$
E(\{\boldsymbol{R}_A\}) = \int F(\boldsymbol{r}; \{\boldsymbol{R}_A\}) \, d\boldsymbol{r} \simeq \sum_{g} w_g F(\boldsymbol{r}_g; \{\boldsymbol{R}_A\})
$$

Differentiating this approximate expression gives

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d} E}{\mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{R}_A} \simeq \sum_{g} w_g \frac{\partial F}{\partial \boldsymbol{R}_A}
$$

When the grid $g$ is complete, the expression above is exact. This is why grid-shift derivatives can be neglected in the usual hybrid GGA gradient calculations.

In reality, however, the number of grid points is finite; moreover, **the grid coordinates $\boldsymbol{r}_g$ in fact depend on the atomic coordinates $\{ \boldsymbol{R}_A \}$** (the grid coordinates are built from Lebedev spherical grids expanded around atoms as centers). Meanwhile, **the grid weights $w_g$ also depend on the atomic coordinates $\{ \boldsymbol{R}_A \}$** (the Lebedev grid weights themselves are fixed, but the weights are additionally scaled by the Becke partition). Therefore we have

$$
E(\{\boldsymbol{R}_A\}) \simeq \sum_{g} w_g(\{\boldsymbol{R}_A\}) F(\boldsymbol{r}_g(\{\boldsymbol{R}_A\}); \{\boldsymbol{R}_A\})
$$

Differentiating this expression gives

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d} E}{\mathrm{d} \boldsymbol{R}_A} \simeq \sum_{g} \left( w_g \frac{\partial F}{\partial \boldsymbol{R}_A} + \frac{\partial w_g}{\partial \boldsymbol{R}_A} F + w_g \frac{\partial F}{\partial \boldsymbol{r}_g} \frac{\partial \boldsymbol{r}_g}{\partial \boldsymbol{R}_A} \right)
$$

Compared with the previous approximate expression, two additional terms appear here: one is the derivative of the grid weights, and the other is the derivative of the grid coordinates. We collectively refer to these two terms as **grid-shift derivatives**.

:::{warning}
**Grid-shift derivatives comprise two very different contributions, not just the grid weight**

This is a misconception I personally held for a long time; namely, that the gradient induced by the grid shift contains only the grid-weight derivative contribution. The misconception arises because the grid weights appear explicitly in the formula, whereas the dependence of the grid coordinates on the atomic coordinates is implicit.
:::

In the usual Lebedev-derived DFT grids, the grid point $g$ is always generated by a specific atom associated with it. If the grid point $g$ is generated by atom $A$, then

$$
\frac{\partial \boldsymbol{r}_g}{\partial \boldsymbol{R}_B} = \delta_{AB} \boldsymbol{I}, \quad g \in A
$$

Here the concept of grid-shift derivatives has been introduced through first-order gradients; grid-shift derivatives for second-order gradients will be discussed in later sections.

### 1.2 Verification Device: Summation over Atomic Indices of the Grad/Hess

:::{warning}
**The verification device is a necessary but not sufficient condition**

If the grid-shift derivatives are implemented correctly, the summation of the Grad/Hess over the atomic indices should be zero; but the converse does not hold.
:::

Grid-shift derivatives themselves are often very small: for GGA first-order gradient problems, the error they induce is often at the level of 10<sup>-6</sup> Hartree/Bohr; but for MGGA second-order gradient problems, they often induce frequency errors of tens to hundreds of cm<sup>-1</sup>. Verifying their correctness is a rather delicate matter.

For first-order gradients, we introduce the translational-invariance verification. Specifically, let the energy be a function of the atomic coordinates $E(\{\boldsymbol{R}_A\})$; if we translate all atomic coordinates by $\boldsymbol{R}_A \to \boldsymbol{R}_A + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}$, the energy should be unchanged. Thus we have

$$
\frac{\partial E (\boldsymbol{R}_A + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \boldsymbol{R}_B + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}, \ldots)}{\partial \boldsymbol{\epsilon}} = \frac{\partial E}{\partial \boldsymbol{R}_A} + \frac{\partial E}{\partial \boldsymbol{R}_B} + \cdots = 0
$$

This equality holds for all orientations of $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$; if we then set $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ to $t \in \{ x, y, z \}$, we obtain three independent equations. The following equation is exactly the verification device of summing the gradient over the atomic indices:

$$
\sum_A \frac{\partial E}{\partial R_{At}} = 0, \quad t \in \{ x, y, z \}
$$

The same applies to second-order gradients:

$$
\sum_{A B} \frac{\partial^2 E}{\partial R_{At} \partial R_{Bs}} = 0, \quad t, s \in \{ x, y, z \}
$$

In actual implementations there will certainly be machine errors; but such errors generally do not exceed 1e-13 Hartree/Bohr for first-order gradients, and 1e-9 Hartree/Bohr<sup>2</sup> for second-order gradients.

The above verification device applies not only to the ordinary Hessian, but also to the contributions specific to skeleton derivatives, as well as to Fock matrix derivatives.

## 2. Review of DFT Energy Grid-Shift First-Order Skeleton Gradients

We now explain the matter with the rather convenient notation of the gradient formulas. The notation here will differ from the previous section; in particular, $R_{At}$ becomes $A_t$.

In the notation used for the program derivation, we restate the review of the first-order skeleton gradient.

$$
\partial_{A_t} E = \sum_g w_g \frac{\partial (f \rho)_g}{\partial A_t} + \sum_g (\partial_{A_t} w_g) f_g \rho_g + \sum_g w_g \frac{\partial (f \rho)_g}{\partial t} \delta_{g \in A}
$$

- Term 1 is the conventional gradient contribution; this is also the part that ordinary gradient programs need to implement.
- Term 2 is the grid-weight shift-derivative contribution; once $\partial_{A_t} w_g$ has been obtained, implementing this term is very straightforward.
- Term 3 is the grid-coordinate shift-derivative contribution.

It is necessary here to discuss the connection between Term 1 and Term 3.

We first examine Term 1. This document is mainly about second-order gradients, and the derivation for first-order gradients has not been presented before; we show it briefly here. Its implementation is roughly derived as:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_{A_t} E
&\leftarrow \sum_{g \chi} w_g \frac{\partial (f \rho)_g}{\partial \xi^\chi_g} \frac{\partial \xi^\chi_g}{\partial A_t}
= \sum_{g \chi} w_g f^\chi_g \frac{\partial \xi^\chi_g}{\partial A_t}
\\
&= \sum_{g \chi} \sum_{\mu \nu} \left( w_g f^\chi_g \frac{\partial \xi_{g \mu \nu}^\chi}{\partial t} D_{\mu \nu} \times - \delta_{\mu \in A} + \mathrm{swap}(\mu, \nu) \right)
\quad (\text{restrict $\partial$ to $\mu$})
\end{aligned}
$$

Swapping the order of summation and introducing the 2-dim temporary tensor $\mathscr{T}_{\mu}^t$:

$$
\mathscr{T}_{\mu}^t = \sum_{g \chi \nu} w_g f^\chi_g \frac{\partial \xi_{g \mu \nu}^\chi}{\partial t} D_{\mu \nu} \quad (\text{restrict $\partial$ to $\mu$})
$$

The ordinary-term derivative of the first-order skeleton gradient can then be written as (the factor of 2 comes from $\mathrm{swap}(\mu, \nu)$):

$$
\partial_{A_t} E \leftarrow - 2 \sum_{\mu \in A} \mathscr{T}_{\mu}^t
$$

How to implement $\mathscr{T}_{\mu}^t$ is the single greatest difficulty of the whole program; but for the discussion of the grid-shift problem it is not the main point. We now examine the expression of Term 3, the grid-coordinate shift-derivative contribution:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\partial_{A_t} E &\leftarrow \sum_{g \chi} w_g f^\chi_g \frac{\partial \xi_g^\chi}{\partial t} \delta_{g \in A} \\
\\
&= \sum_{g \chi} \sum_{\mu \nu} w_g f_g^\chi \frac{\partial \xi_{g \mu \nu}^\chi}{\partial t} D_{\mu \nu} \delta_{g \in A} + \mathrm{swap}(\mu, \nu)
\quad (\text{restrict $\partial$ to $\mu$})
\end{aligned}
$$

There are two computational strategies here:
- compute $\partial_t \xi_g^\chi$ fully decoupled, and then perform a simple contraction;
- do not decouple $\partial_t \xi_{g \mu \nu}^\chi$, and perform a rather more complicated contraction.

Both strategies occur in the second-order gradient program; but in the first-order gradient program, the second strategy is generally the better choice:
- computing $\partial_t \xi_g^\chi$ fully decoupled is itself a cost. Its meaning is perfectly clear, but its computational complexity and workload are no smaller than those of $\mathscr{T}_{\mu}^t$.

This does not mean, however, that the previous program can be reused painlessly as is. We must modify the computation of $\mathscr{T}_{\mu}^t$, introducing the 3-dim tensor $\mathscr{T}_{\mu}^{A t}$ at the level of the formula:

$$
\mathscr{T}_{\mu}^{A t} = \sum_{g \chi \nu} w_g f^\chi_g \frac{\partial \xi_{g \mu \nu}^\chi}{\partial t} D_{\mu \nu} \delta_{g \in A} \quad (\text{restrict $\partial$ to $\mu$})
$$

It really just splits the summation result over the grid according to which atom $A$ generated the Lebedev grid that the grid point $g$ belongs to. The process is neither complicated nor difficult to achieve; but it does impose the following data-structure requirement:

:::{admonition} Implementation decision: grids sorted by atom first
:class: note

Rationale: the computation of $\mathscr{T}_{\mu}^{A t}$ involves matrix multiplication, whose multiplied-over index is the grid $g$. This index delivers respectable performance only when reasonably good contiguity is guaranteed.

Cost: our DFT integration program is still matmul-based; but for very large molecular or solid systems, the more appropriate approach is to sparsify to some degree according to the distance between grid points and atomic-orbital centers. The contiguity that sparsification requires is that the spatial coordinates $\boldsymbol{r}_g$ associated with the grid points $g$ be close, not whether $g$ was generated by the Lebedev grid of the same atom. Note that Lebedev grids often extend to rather large radii (up to possibly 10 Angstrom), so Lebedev grids generated by different atoms may overlap.

One possible future solution is, after grouping by atom in advance, to further sort by spatial coordinates (something close to a combination of bucket sorts). This is not entirely trivial, because in general the simplest way to generate DFT grids is to iterate over atoms first, then over radii, and finally produce the grid from tabulated Lebedev data; there is clearly a little room for optimization in the spatial ordering. But this is not an implementation goal of the current document and program.
:::

In the concrete implementation, we always batch the grid points. Hence the program originally used to compute $\mathscr{T}_{\mu}^t$ can be reused: once the $\mathscr{T}_{\mu}^{A t}$ increment for one chunk of grid points (which must be Lebedev grids generated by the same atom) is obtained, and after accumulating it into the Term-1 ordinary skeleton derivative contribution, do not rush to discard it:

$$
\partial_{A_t} E \leftarrow - 2 \sum_{\mu \in A} \sum_B \mathscr{T}_{\mu}^{B t}
$$

(Although, as a formula, the expression above sums over $B$; in practice one chunk of grid points always consists solely of Lebedev grid points generated by one and the same atom, so in the actual program this manifests as always incrementing this 2-dim tensor)

We can immediately reuse this tensor as well, to implement the Term-3 grid-coordinate shift skeleton derivative contribution:

$$
\partial_{A_t} E \leftarrow 2 \sum_\mu \mathscr{T}_{\mu}^{A t}
$$

## 3. Implementation of DFT Energy Grid-Shift Second-Order Skeleton Gradients

### 3.1 Grid Density Derivatives and Their Use Cases

| Variable | Meaning | Index order | Shape | Notes |
|--|--|--|--|--|
| `drho` | $\partial_{A_t} \xi_g^\chi$ | $(g, \chi, t, A)$<br>`[g, x, t, A]` | `[ngrids, nvar, 3, natm]` | |
| `prho` | $\partial_{t} \xi_g^\chi$<br>(= $-\sum_A \partial_{A_t} \xi_g^\chi$) | $(g, \chi, t)$<br>`[g, x, t]` | `[ngrids, nvar, 3]` | |

We recall that $\partial_{A_t} = - \partial_t \delta_{\mu \in A}$; summing $\partial_{A_t}$ over the atomic index $A$ immediately gives the relation for $\partial_t$:

$$
\partial_t \xi_g^\chi = - \sum_A \partial_{A_t} \xi_g^\chi
$$

```rust
let prho = -drho.sum_axes(3);
```

Since this computation is essentially free, and `drho` $\partial_{A_t} \xi_g^\chi$ must be computed anyway in the second-order gradient program, we can immediately reuse it to obtain `prho` $\partial_t \xi_g^\chi$, thereby simplifying part of the grid-coordinate shift skeleton derivatives.

But it should also be pointed out that the whole second-order gradient program does not compute $\partial_{A_t} \partial_{B_s} \xi_g^\chi$, i.e., second derivatives of the density on grids: on the one hand the computational cost is too high, and on the other hand this is a rather large $O(N^3)$ memory requirement. Therefore, whenever second-order density derivatives are needed, one must expand the density in atomic orbitals and compute them in a more complicated way (which nevertheless reuses the ordinary second-order gradient intermediates).

### 3.2 Overview of Grid-Shift Second-Order Skeleton Derivatives

We present here the complete expression of the second-order gradient:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial^2 E^\text{xc}}{\partial A_t \partial B_s}
&= \sum_{g \chi \chi'} w_g f_g^{\chi \chi'} \frac{\partial \xi_g^\chi}{\partial A_t} \frac{\partial \xi_g^{\chi'}}{\partial B_s}
&&\quad \texttt{de\_fxc} \\
&+ \sum_{g \chi} w_g f_g^\chi \frac{\partial^2 \xi_g^\chi}{\partial A_t \partial B_s}
&&\quad \texttt{de\_vxc} \\
&+ \sum_{g \chi} \frac{\partial w_g}{\partial A_t} f_g^\chi \frac{\partial \xi_g^\chi}{\partial B_s} + \mathrm{swap}(A_t, B_s)
&&\quad \texttt{de\_becke\_full\_1} \\
&+ \sum_{g} \frac{\partial^2 w_g}{\partial A_t \partial B_s} f_g \rho_g
&&\quad \texttt{de\_becke\_full\_2} \\
&+ \sum_{g \chi \chi'} w_g f_g^{\chi \chi'} \frac{\partial \xi_g^\chi}{\partial t} \frac{\partial \xi_g^{\chi'}}{\partial B_s} \delta_{g \in A} + \mathrm{swap}(A_t, B_s)
&&\quad \texttt{de\_becke\_atom\_1} \\
&+ \sum_{g \chi} \frac{\partial w_g}{\partial B_s} f_g^\chi \frac{\partial \xi_g^\chi}{\partial t} \delta_{g \in A} + \mathrm{swap}(A_t, B_s)
&&\quad \texttt{de\_becke\_atom\_2} \\
&+ \sum_{g \chi \chi'} w_g f_g^{\chi \chi'} \frac{\partial \xi_g^\chi}{\partial t} \frac{\partial \xi_g^{\chi'}}{\partial s} \delta_{g \in A}
&&\quad \texttt{de\_becke\_atom\_3} \\
&+ \sum_{g \chi} w_g f_g^\chi \frac{\partial^2 \xi_g^\chi}{\partial t \, \partial B_s} \delta_{g \in A} + \mathrm{swap}(A_t, B_s)
&&\quad \\
&+ \sum_{g \chi} w_g f_g^\chi \frac{\partial^2 \xi_g^\chi}{\partial t \partial s} \delta_{g \in A} \delta_{A B}
&&\quad \texttt{de\_becke\_vxc}
\end{aligned}
$$

Among these terms,
- `de_fxc`, `de_vxc` are the ordinary second-order gradient contributions.
- The four terms `de_becke_full_1`, `de_becke_atom_1`, `de_becke_atom_2`, `de_becke_atom_3` can be computed immediately with einsum based on the intermediates of the ordinary second-order gradient (together with the grid-weight gradient supplied by the Becke partition). The use of einsum is not recommended in the official REST program; these terms are implemented in the actual program through multi-step vecdot.
- The `de_becke_full_2` term is rather special. It involves the second-order weight gradient $\partial_{A_t} \partial_{B_s} w_g$, which is $O(N^3)$ if stored. Even with batching over the grid, it would still occupy a considerable memory footprint. In the actual program we perform a fused contraction: the tensor to be contracted, $f_g \rho_g$, is supplied in advance, and a Hessian increment of shape $(A, B, t, s)$ is returned.
- The `de_becke_vxc` term (two lines) is the part involving second derivatives of the density on grids; we avoid generating such tensors directly. This means we need to reuse the computation process of the intermediate tensors therein.
- The $\delta_{AB}$ in the last line arises because the grid derivative there requires both $\delta_{g \in A}$ and $\delta_{g \in B}$, so there is a contribution only when $A = B$. In the formula derivations below, using $\delta_{AB}$ is more convenient.

### 3.3 Implementation of the `de_becke_vxc` Contribution

`de_becke_vxc` is the only one of the eight contributions that involves second derivatives of the density on grids; it contains the mixed second-order $\partial_t \partial_{B_s} \xi_g^\chi$ and the purely spatial second-order $\partial_t \partial_s \xi_g^\chi$. The computational cost of these two terms is a very large $O(N^3)$; ideally they are not generated directly, but computed by reusing the ordinary second-order gradient intermediates (c.f. Section 4.2 `dao_vxc_diag` and Section 5.1 `dao_vxc_off` of the [ordinary second-order skeleton derivatives](skeleton2.md) document).

We first review the intermediates `dao_vxc_diag` and `dao_vxc_off`. Unlike the earlier notation, we now introduce the summation over the grid points of a specific atom:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathscr{T}_{\mu}^{A(ts)} &= 2 \sum_{g \in A} \sum_{\chi \nu} w_g f_g^\chi \frac{\partial^2 \xi_{g \mu \nu}^\chi}{\partial t \partial s} D_{\mu \nu}
&&\quad \text{(restrict $\partial$ to $\mu$, \texttt{dao\_vxc\_diag})} \\
\mathscr{T}_{\mu \nu}^{A t s} &= \sum_{g \in A} \sum_{\chi} w_g f_g^\chi \frac{\partial^2 \xi_{g \mu \nu}^\chi}{\partial t \partial s} + \text{swap} (t \mu, s \nu)
&&\quad \text{(restrict $\partial_t$ to $\mu$, $\partial_s$ to $\nu$, \texttt{dao\_vxc\_off})}
\end{aligned}
$$

The relation between these two temporary tensors and the final second-order skeleton gradient is

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial^2 E^\text{xc}}{\partial A_t \partial B_s}
&\leftarrow \sum_{g \chi} w_g f_g^\chi \frac{\partial^2 \xi_g^\chi}{\partial t \, \partial B_s} \delta_{g \in A} + \mathrm{swap} (A_t, B_s) \\
&= - \sum_{\mu \in B} \Big( \mathscr{T}_{\mu}^{A(ts)} + \sum_\nu \mathscr{T}_{\mu \nu}^{A st} D_{\mu \nu} \Big) + \mathrm{swap} (A_t, B_s) \\
\frac{\partial^2 E^\text{xc}}{\partial A_t \partial B_s}
&\leftarrow \sum_{g \chi} w_g f_g^\chi \frac{\partial^2 \xi_g^\chi}{\partial t \, \partial s} \delta_{g \in A} \delta_{A B} \\
&= \frac{1}{2} \delta_{A B} \sum_{\mu} \Big( \mathscr{T}_{\mu}^{A(ts)} + \sum_\nu \mathscr{T}_{\mu \nu}^{A ts} D_{\mu \nu} \Big) + \mathrm{swap} (A_t, B_s)
\end{aligned}
$$

We deliberately introduce the $\frac{1}{2}$ coefficient in the $\partial_t \partial_s \xi_g^\chi$ term, solely to match the $\mathrm{swap} (A_t, B_s)$ used for $\partial_t \partial_{B_s} \xi_g^\chi$.

Accordingly, we reorganize the computation of `de_becke_vxc` as:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial^2 E^\text{xc}}{\partial A_t \partial B_s}
&\leftarrow \frac{1}{2} \delta_{AB} \sum_{\mu} \mathscr{T}_{\mu}^{A(ts)} - \sum_{\mu \in B} \mathscr{T}_{\mu}^{A(ts)}
&&\quad \texttt{de\_becke\_vxc\_diag} \\
&+ \frac{1}{2} \delta_{AB} \sum_{\mu \nu} \mathscr{T}_{\mu \nu}^{A ts} D_{\mu \nu} - \sum_{\mu \in B} \sum_\nu \mathscr{T}_{\mu \nu}^{A st} D_{\mu \nu}
&&\quad \texttt{de\_becke\_vxc\_off} \\
&+ \mathrm{swap} (A_t, B_s)
\end{aligned}
$$

Note that the implementation has a few aspects that the formulas can hardly express clearly:
- This function is carried out under batching over the grid points $g$; one chunk of grid points always corresponds to the Lebedev grid of one and the same atom $A$. Consequently, none of the $A$ indices here appear in the shapes.
- Although the program outputs `de_becke_vxc_diag/off`, they have not been symmetrized by $\mathrm{swap} (A_t, B_s)$: each chunk's output is a $(t, s, B)$ increment with the direction axes interchanged, which is scattered onto the last (B) axis of the full Hessian of shape $(t, s, A, B)$ (i.e., the column atom is the generating atom of the chunk); once all chunks have been accumulated, the $\mathrm{swap} (A_t, B_s)$ symmetrization is applied all at once.

There is an identically structured computation in the gradient evaluation; the function `contract_pvxc` exists precisely to handle that structure:

$$
\mathscr{P}_{t s}^B = \frac{1}{2} \delta_{AB} \sum_{\mu} \mathscr{S}_\mu^{t s} - \sum_{\mu \in B} \mathscr{S}_\mu^{t s}
$$

For the two cases diag/off, the $\mathscr{S}_\mu^{t s}$ here corresponds respectively to

$$
\mathscr{S}_\mu^{t s} = \begin{cases}
\mathscr{T}_\mu^{A (t s)} \delta_{AB}, & \texttt{diag} \\
\sum_\nu \mathscr{T}_{\mu \nu}^{A ts} D_{\mu \nu}, & \texttt{off}
\end{cases}
$$

There is one point here that is rather easy to find confusing. The tensor here is $\mathscr{P}_{ts}^B$; on its face it contains no index $A$, but in reality it is computed from the batch/chunk of grid points generated by atom $A$, and must serve that particular atom $A$. This is also why `atm_idx` is still retained among the parameters of the `contract_pvxc` function.

## 4. Implementation of DFT Fock Grid-Shift First-Order Skeleton Gradients

### 4.1 Overview of the Grid-Shift Fock First-Order Skeleton Gradient

We present here the complete expression of the first-order gradient of the Fock matrix:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V_{\mu \nu}^\text{xc} &= \sum_{g \chi} w_g f_g^\chi \xi_{g \mu \nu}^\chi
\\
\frac{\partial V_{\mu \nu}^\text{xc}}{\partial A_t}
&= \sum_{g \chi \chi'} w_g f_g^{\chi \chi'} \frac{\partial \xi_g^{\chi'}}{\partial A_t} \xi_{g \mu \nu}^\chi
&&\quad \texttt{vmat\_fxc} \\
&+ \sum_{g \chi} w_g f_g^\chi \frac{\partial \xi_{g \mu \nu}^\chi}{\partial A_t}
&&\quad \texttt{vmat\_vxc} \\
&+ \sum_{g \chi} \frac{\partial w_g}{\partial A_t} f_g^\chi \xi_{g \mu \nu}^\chi
&&\quad \texttt{vmat\_becke\_dw} \\
&+ \sum_{g \chi \chi'} w_g f_g^{\chi \chi'} \frac{\partial \xi_g^{\chi'}}{\partial t} \xi_{g \mu \nu}^\chi \delta_{g \in A}
&&\quad \texttt{vmat\_becke\_fxc} \\
&+ \sum_{g \chi} w_g f_g^\chi \frac{\partial \xi_{g \mu \nu}^\chi}{\partial t} \delta_{g \in A}
&&\quad \texttt{vmat\_becke\_vxc}
\end{aligned}
$$

Among them, `vmat_fxc` and `vmat_vxc` are the ordinary Fock first-order gradient contributions; `vmat_becke_dw`, `vmat_becke_fxc` and `vmat_becke_vxc` are the grid-shift derivative contributions.
